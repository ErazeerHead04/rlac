## Introduction

Computational text analysis often relies on visualizations to reveal patterns across large collections of texts. Tools such as Voyant and R make it possible to track word frequencies, thematic clusters, and relationships between characters across multiple documents. These visualizations can appear objective and self-explanatory because they display numerical patterns visually. However, interpreting visualizations without proper context can easily lead to misleading conclusions.

This response explores how context shapes the interpretation of visualizations created in Assignment 1. Using several visualizations generated from a corpus of *Harry Potter* novels and fanfiction, I tested how a large language model (LLM) interprets these visuals with and without contextual information. I first asked the LLM to interpret the graphs without explaining their origin. I then provided additional context about the corpus and the computational methods used to generate them.  

The differences between these responses demonstrate that visualizations rarely “speak for themselves.” Instead, interpretation depends heavily on contextual knowledge, both for human readers and for AI systems.

## Experiment 1: Interpreting a Visualization Without Context

### Figure 1 – Snape Theme Bubblelines

![Snape Bubblelines Visualization](https://erazeerhead04.github.io/rlac/assets/images/mix_snape.png)

The first visualization tracks the distribution of the words **snape, dark, love, lily, and evil** across the corpus. Larger bubbles represent higher word frequency within sections of the texts.

To test how a reader might interpret this graph without context, I asked an LLM to explain the visualization.

### LLM Response Without Context

![LLM Prompt and Response](https://erazeerhead04.github.io/rlac/assets/images/snapeWOcontext.png)

> Explain what this visualization shows. What conclusions could someone draw from it?

### LLM Response

Without any explanation of the dataset or methodology, the LLM interpreted the graph as a narrative representation of Snape’s moral transformation. It suggested that the visualization shows Snape being associated with darkness and evil earlier in the story and later becoming associated with love and Lily, implying a shift from villain to tragic hero.

This interpretation appears plausible because it aligns with the plot of the Harry Potter series. However, it also demonstrates a common risk in visualization interpretation: **the graph itself only shows word frequency patterns**, not narrative meaning. The model inferred a story about character development even though the visualization only displays how often certain words appear across texts.

## Experiment 2: Adding Context

Next, I provided the LLM with additional contextual information about the visualization.


![LLM Response after Context](https://erazeerhead04.github.io/rlac/assets/images/snapeWcontext.png)

> These graphs come from computational text analysis of Harry Potter novels and fanfiction using Voyant Tools. The visualization tracks how often words appear across texts. Given this context, how should we interpret the visualization?

After receiving the additional explanation, the LLM produced a more cautious interpretation. Instead of claiming that the graph directly represents Snape’s moral arc, it explained that the visualization shows **patterns of word usage** and that interpretation requires knowledge of the narrative context.

This shift illustrates how contextual framing changes interpretation. Without context, the LLM treated the visualization as narrative evidence. With context, it recognized that the graph represents **quantitative patterns rather than narrative conclusions**.

## Why Context Matters

These experiments demonstrate that visualizations are highly dependent on contextual framing. Without contextual explanation, viewers may draw conclusions that go beyond what the data actually shows. Visualizations compress complex datasets into simplified forms, and this simplification can remove important contextual information.

Research on misleading visualizations supports this observation. Lisnic et al. (2023) found that most misleading charts are not deceptive because of visual design tricks but because audiences draw incorrect conclusions from incomplete information. In their analysis of thousands of social media posts containing charts, they found that **84% of misleading visualizations involved reasoning errors rather than visual design flaws**.

This insight is highly relevant for computational text analysis. Voyant visualizations reveal patterns in word usage, but they do not explain why those patterns occur. Interpretation requires contextual knowledge about the texts, the corpus selection, and the analytical methods used.

Ted Underwood makes a similar point in *The Risks of Distant Reading*. Quantitative analysis can reveal large-scale patterns that are difficult to observe through traditional close reading, but those patterns still require interpretation. Without careful contextualization, distant reading can lead researchers to over-interpret patterns that may have multiple explanations.

---

## Conclusion

This experiment shows that visualizations do not communicate meaning independently. When the LLM interpreted the graphs without contextual information, it constructed narrative explanations that went beyond the data. Once context was provided, the interpretation became more cautious and methodologically accurate.

Visualizations are powerful tools for identifying patterns across large textual datasets, but they must be accompanied by clear explanations. Context allows readers to understand what the visualization represents, how the data was generated, and what kinds of conclusions can reasonably be drawn.

For computational literary analysis, this means that distant reading and visualization should always be paired with interpretive context. Visualizations are not conclusions in themselves; they are starting points for deeper analysis.
